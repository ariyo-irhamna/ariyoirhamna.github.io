I am a development economist studying how climate shocks affect agricultural livelihoods and health in Southeast Asia and Indonesia. My research integrates high-resolution weather and climate data with household panel surveys, administrative health records, and plant-level industrial data to estimate causal effects using within-household variation in temperature exposure.

Prior to my PhD, I spent more than a decade conducting policy research and advocacy across a wide range of economics topics with senior government officials in Indonesia, based at the [Institute for Development of Economics and Finance (INDEF)](https://indef.or.id/). I regularly provide economic commentary to Indonesian media on current policy issues such as poverty, trade, fiscal policy, and industrial policy.

- The Causal Effect of Temperature on Rice Productivity: Evidence from Tropical Southeast Asia
  <details style="margin-left: 1.5em;">
    <summary class="expand-abstract">Abstract (click to expand)</summary>
    <p>
<<<<<<< HEAD
    This paper estimates the causal effect of temperature on rice yields using a panel of 3,228 rice farmers in Thailand and Vietnam (2007–2017), matching ERA5 daily temperatures to each household's reported growing season. A two-component degree day model with country-specific thresholds (24°C for Thailand, 31°C for Vietnam) separates beneficial from harmful heat. Household, village, and agroecological zone fixed effects and province-specific linear year trends isolate the identifying variation to within-household temperature shocks. One additional harmful degree day reduces log yield by 0.199 in the pooled sample (p < 0.01). Both countries exhibit statistically significant yield losses: −0.103 per harmful degree day in Thailand and −1.795 in Vietnam. Lowland farms bear the brunt of heat damage, while the highland differential is insignificant. Results hold under calendar-fixed weather windows, Conley spatial standard errors, progressive time trend specifications, alternative functional forms, and winsorisation.
=======
    Rice feeds more than half the world's population, and the majority of global production is concentrated in tropical regions where temperatures are rising. This paper estimates the causal effect of temperature on rice yields and crop income in Thailand and Vietnam using household panel data from the Thailand Vietnam Socioeconomic Panel (TVSEP), 2007–2017. I exploit within-household variation in growing-season temperature exposure, driven by year-to-year differences in planting dates that generate different degree day accumulations for the same household. The damage from one day of excessive heat is roughly six times the benefit from one day of moderate warmth. The effects differ markedly across countries: Thai farmers suffer yield losses from harmful heat approximately twice as severe as Vietnamese farmers. Heterogeneity analyses along three dimensions (country, elevation, and province) reveal that the temperature–productivity relationship varies with local agricultural conditions, yet the harmful-heat effect is consistently negative across all subsamples. The results are robust to alternative functional forms, split-sample elevation checks, winsorisation of outliers, and singleton exclusion.

- Rice Farmer Adaptation to Harmful Heat: Causal Evidence from Tropical Southeast Asia
  <details style="margin-left: 1.5em;">
    <summary class="expand-abstract">Abstract (click to expand)</summary>
    <p>
<<<<<<< HEAD
    How do rice farmers respond to extreme heat in the short run? Using a panel of 3,228 rice-farming households in Thailand and Vietnam (2007–2017), I exploit within-household temperature variation to identify behavioural responses across six input and land-use margins. I match each adaptation channel to its decision-relevant treatment window, using pre-planting heat for land allocation and growing-season heat for input adjustment, separating anticipatory from reactive responses. One additional harmful degree day before planting increases rice area by 32 per cent. Growing-season heat reduces fertiliser expenditure by 16.5 per cent and increases irrigation expenditure by 26 per cent per harmful degree day. Crop diversification shows no response. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that rice area expansion requires land, not income: only farms with above-median agricultural land expand, while smallholders compensate through irrigation. Input reallocation, not structural crop transformation, is the primary margin of short-run response to heat, and land access determines which households can adjust. Results are robust to calendar-fixed weather windows, district-specific time trends, and Oster bounds.
    </p>
  </details>

- Land Allocation as Climate Adaptation: Evidence from Rice Farmers in Thailand and Vietnam
  <details style="margin-left: 1.5em;">
    <summary class="expand-abstract">Abstract (click to expand)</summary>
    <p>
    The companion paper (Paper 2) documents that rice farmers expand rice area in response to pre-planting heat. This paper investigates the extensive margin: how do households acquire the additional land? Using the same TVSEP panel, I decompose total land holdings into acquisition channels (inherited, purchased, government-allocated, and other) and estimate how pre-planting harmful heat affects each channel. One additional harmful degree day increases total landholding by 0.60 hectares in the pooled sample. The dominant channel is inherited land (+0.27 ha), followed by purchased land (+0.06 ha). Thailand drives the pooled result, with inherited (+0.41 ha) and purchased (+0.09 ha) land both significant. In Vietnam, government-allocated land is the only significant channel (+0.29 ha). These findings reveal that the rice area expansion documented in Paper 2 operates through genuine land acquisition, not reallocation of existing holdings, and that the institutional channel differs across countries.

- Flooding and Health: Causal Evidence from Indonesia's National Health Insurance
=======
    I study how farmers adjust land allocation and input decisions in response to temperature using the same TVSEP panel. Farmers adapt through two channels: land allocation before planting and input expenditure during the growing season. Harmful heat triggers rice area expansion and higher input spending, while moderate warmth leads to reduced area and lower expenditure. Vietnamese farmers show a stronger extensive-margin response than Thai farmers. Elevation moderates adaptation, with highland farms expanding rice area more aggressively under heat stress. The land-use decomposition reveals that adjustments operate primarily through rice and residential land categories. The results are robust to alternative functional forms, split-sample elevation, and first-difference estimation.
    </p>
  </details>

- Behavioural Mechanisms in Climate Adaptation: The Causal Effects of Weather on Private Irrigation Decision-Making
  <details style="margin-left: 1.5em;">
    <summary class="expand-abstract">Abstract (click to expand)</summary>
    <p>
    Using panel data from 17,443 household-year observations in rural Thailand and Vietnam (2007–2017), I estimate a correlated random effects logit model with an autoregressive distributed lag structure to identify causal effects of weather on irrigation adoption. The results show predominantly reactive adaptation: farmers respond to contemporaneous weather conditions, with prior irrigation experience strongly predicting current adoption, while lagged weather signals have no significant effect.
